Little Mountain Service Area

A District Built for No One

A water district created for zero residents, run by the same handful of landowning families for nearly two decades, became the financial pass-through for the largest development project in Weber County's history — and the people governing it were, at times, the same people benefiting from it.

I. 2007

A district with no one to answer to

The Little Mountain Service Area was created to provide sewer service to the far western edge of Weber County — agricultural land, mostly, with almost no rooftops on it. The county's own resolution creating its first board of trustees says so plainly.

Seven seats were filled that day. Two-year terms, renewable, no election required — because there was, by the county's own account, no electorate to hold one for.

The pivot

Eleven months after the county's first quiet resolution, the county's own Economic Development Director stood up and explained, on the record, exactly where Little Mountain's leftover federal sewer money was going.

“This contract will outline the details of the ARPA funding for the Sewer Lagoon Improvement System and the Wastewater Improvement System in west Weber… Any remaining funds from the LMSA Lagoon Rehabilitation effort will be sent to this program, with the County acting as pass-through for those funds.”
— Stephanie Russell, Weber County Commission meeting, June 25, 2024

At that same meeting, on the same agenda, the county appointed a developer — a man who would, twenty days later, propose that Little Mountain take over his own subdivision's sewer system — to the board governing the district whose money Russell had just described.

At that June 25, 2024 meeting, Stephanie Russell held three distinct roles at once — the person deciding where the money went, the person running the district that had it, and a person paid by that district to represent its interests in the room.

V. Why It Matters

Who actually gets a say

Utah law waives the public-hearing requirement for a special-district annexation when the person asking is the only property owner affected. On paper, that makes sense — if you own land and nobody else does, there's no one else to hear from.

But that rule assumes the person asking is an ordinary landowner, not someone who already sits on the board deciding whether to say yes. When the petitioner and the decision-maker are the same person, the "no one else to hear from" logic breaks down — the one person whose voice matters most is also the one voice the process was built to check.

The public doesn't get a vote on Little Mountain's board. It never has — the district was built for a place with no residents, and residency is still not how a seat is earned. What the public gets, in theory, is the hearing: the moment where a decision affecting shared infrastructure has to be explained out loud, in public, before it's final. That's the mechanism that's absent here, twice, for the same board member's own land.

None of this required breaking a rule. That's arguably the more uncomfortable version of the story: a district that was already structurally insulated from public input used a second, entirely legal shortcut to remove the one moment of public input that remained.

A district built for zero residents, run by the same handful of landowning families for close to two decades, became the vehicle for financing the largest development project in Weber County's history. One meeting — June 25, 2024 — put a county official on every side of that district's money, and put a developer on its board who would, within a year, ask it to take over his own infrastructure costs and annex his own land into it without the hearing an outside owner would have faced. None of it required breaking a rule. All of it is on the public record, in each participant's own name.
