What They Were Warned About

Before the West Weber Inland Port was adopted, more than 1,500 people signed a petition against it, a retired environmental engineer asked directly for a delay, and the project's own review gave the land's Tribal history a single sentence. None of it changed the outcome.

One Sentence

What the Plan says about Tribal lands

UIPA's own Project Area Plan requires an environmental review covering "Historical and Cultural Resources including Tribal Lands." For West Weber, here is that finding in its entirety.

WEST WEBER PROJECT AREA PLANCULTURAL RESOURCES
“Northwestern Band of the Shoshone Nation is located in the vicinity of the project area.”
— The complete tribal-lands finding, West Weber Project Area Plan

That single sentence is the entire tribal review. By comparison, wetlands and wildlife receive several pages of the same document, with specific buffer requirements, species lists, and dedicated funding.

A separate federal filing offers a useful contrast. PacifiCorp's licensing consultation for its Pioneer Hydroelectric Project — a different facility, in the same county — names four tribes with potential cultural or religious connection to the land (Northwestern Shoshone, Ute, Goshute, and Paiute), and sends direct written notice to each tribal chair by name. West Weber's own review names one tribe, once, with no documented direct notice.

The tribe's own Vice Chairman, Brad Parry, was quoted specifically on West Weber in a March 2025 report — and his account draws a precise distinction worth having exactly right: UIPA asked about cultural resources, but did not consult on wetlands specifically. That's a narrower gap than "no outreach at all," and it's worth stating precisely rather than overstating it.

Fairness note: an earlier claim that UIPA "hides" or omits this history from its own review does not hold up — the Plan contains a dedicated Cultural Resources and Tribal Lands section that discloses the tribe's proximity directly, by name. What's thin here is the depth of consultation and detail, not disclosure itself. The tribal contact of record is Chairman Dennis Alex, elected April 2020.

What's already there

The Plan's own environmental review identifies specific species already using this land — not hypothetical habitat, but documented presence within a two-mile radius of the project boundary.

Among the nine: American White Pelican, Bald Eagle, Caspian Tern, Kit Fox, Northern Leopard Frog, Peregrine Falcon, Snowy Plover, White-faced Ibis, and Winged Floater. Wilson's Phalarope, also found here, is currently petitioned for federal Endangered Species Act listing.

The Plan's own review adds two more findings: Weber County sits in serious nonattainment for PM2.5 and moderate nonattainment for 8-hour ozone, and the project area carries a 1% annual chance flood hazard — both cited directly from federal data in the Plan itself.

What we checked and could not confirm

Two open items, stated plainly

This investigation began with a claim that the project was built on Native American burial grounds. No source has been found documenting a specific burial site on this footprint — that claim remains unverified, and it is not repeated here as fact.

Separately, sources disagree on distance: the Plan states the tribe's location as roughly 20 miles northeast of the project area; a March 2025 report puts tribal headquarters roughly 7 miles east. The discrepancy likely reflects two different reference points — a regional office versus tribal headquarters — rather than an error in either source, and it remains unreconciled.

The engineer's letter

Four days before the vote, a retired environmental engineer wrote directly to UIPA's executive director. Not a form letter — a technical letter from someone with a specific, relevant career: ten years managing environmental contracts for Kennecott, the first LEED-accredited professional in Utah.

LETTER TO BEN HART, UIPA EXECUTIVE DIRECTORMAY 16, 2024
“Please exercise your awareness of these values by removing consideration of the Weber County Utah Inland Port from the agenda of the May 20, 2024 UIPA Meeting.”
— Ivan Weber, Weber Sustainability Consulting

His letter raised a second, separate claim worth checking directly: that a photo on UIPA's own website, presented as representing the project area's wetlands, actually shows Phragmites — an invasive reed that displaces the native plants wildlife depend on.

What happened next
4
days between the letter and the vote

The agenda item wasn't removed

On May 20, 2024, the UIPA Board adopted the West Weber Project Area Plan. The petition, the letter, and the request to delay all came before the vote. None of them changed its outcome.

None of this proves the board acted in bad faith — boards routinely receive opposition on projects that ultimately move forward. What the record does show is that the volume and specificity of the warnings were real: over 1,500 named signatures, a technical letter from a career environmental engineer, and a direct, dated request to simply wait. The vote happened on schedule anyway.
