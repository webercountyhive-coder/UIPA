Who Decides, Part 4: The Inland Port. Who writes its rules, and who checks it.

The Utah Inland Port Authority (UIPA) is a state authority run by an appointed board. Here is who sits on it, every bill that changed its powers from 2018 to 2026 with its sponsors and votes, how each change grades for the public, and the 2027 bills to watch.

Agency: Utah Inland Port Authority Board: 5 voting, 3 nonvoting, appointed Bills checked: 12 UIPA bills, 2 related, 1 died

Posted Nov. 18, 2026 · Updated Nov. 18, 2026

WHERE
Statewide · 16 project areas, including the Northwest Quadrant (Salt Lake City), West Weber (Weber County), Golden Spike (Box Elder County) and Tooele Valley (Tooele County)
WHO
  • UIPA board: 5 voting members. The governor appoints 3, the Senate president 1 and the House speaker 1 (Utah Code 11-58-302)

The short version

  • The Legislature created the Utah Inland Port Authority in 2018. Its board is appointed, not elected. It now has 16 project areas around the state.
  • From 2018 to 2026, 12 bills that changed UIPA passed. Sen. Jerry Stevenson, who sits on UIPA's board, was the primary sponsor of 6 and the Senate sponsor of 2 more. The law bars only the governor's three picks from being elected officials; it does not bar the Senate president's or speaker's picks.
  • On its own land, UIPA receives 75% of the growth in property tax for 25 years, and its board can add up to 15. In other project areas it can receive up to 75%, set by the board (11-58-601).
  • Some changes reduced what owners and the public can decide or see: owners lost the right to consent and got a 45-day window to ask to be left out (2023); business proposals can be protected records while UIPA evaluates them (2025).
  • Some changes added public steps: a procurement policy and Executive Appropriations approval for loans (2023), a yearly review of UIPA's law (2025), and disclosure before tax increment deals and bonds (2026, S.B. 206).
  • 2024 barred distribution centers on contaminated land. 2025 allowed them if a private landowner owns the land.
  • Four of the bills became public with 7 to 17 days left in the session.

Start here: what UIPA is

The Legislature created the Utah Inland Port Authority in 2018 (S.B. 234) to develop a logistics hub in Salt Lake City's Northwest Quadrant. Since 2019, it can also create project areas in other parts of the state.

Inside a project area, property owners pay the full tax rate and the county treasurer collects it. Then the agreed share of the growth in property tax, for the agreed term, goes to UIPA instead of to the taxing entities' budgets. UIPA uses it for roads, rail, water and other projects in the area. A county or city must give written consent before land goes into a project area (11-58-501).

For a plain-language walkthrough, see What Is UIPA? (UI 100.01). The words used here are explained in Words to know.

How it measures up

Helps
5

Bill-Tracker letter (C, C−, D)
  • Transparency. 2019 set a time limit for challenging a project area. 2021 added an Open Meetings exception (for Point of the Mountain). 2023 added UIPA to the tax-information nondisclosure rules. 2025 let business proposals be protected records during evaluation. 2026 S.B. 225 moved setting trigger dates from a board resolution to the executive director's written order, and 2026 H.B. 507 did the same for nondisclosure agreements. On the other side, 2025 added a yearly review of UIPA's law, and 2026 S.B. 206 added disclosure before deals and yearly reports.
  • Financial accountability. 2020 sent 25% of the growth in UIPA's own land back to local taxing entities. 2023 required a procurement policy and Executive Appropriations approval for loans. 2024 barred paying certain developer costs. Spending powers also grew: business recruitment incentives (2022), PIDs and assessments (2021), and funding for land next to a project area (2025).
  • Environmental impact. 2020 allowed spending on lower-impact development and zero-emissions logistics. 2024 barred distribution centers on contaminated land; 2025 allowed them on privately owned contaminated land. 2026 S.B. 225 replaced the 200,000-gallon daily water limit for incentives with the local water provider's limit.
  • Local say. Counties and cities still must consent in writing. Since 2023, landowners can't block inclusion; they can ask in writing, within 45 days, to be left out.

One example, West Weber: the plan sets the share at 75% to UIPA and 25% to the local taxing entities, for 25 years, with up to 15 more. Three percent of UIPA's share goes to wetland mitigation. The amended plan (June 25, 2026, 8,967.74 acres) estimates UIPA's share at about $343 million over 25 years.

What went right (from the records)

  • Money back to local budgets. Since 2020, at least 25% of the growth in UIPA's own land stays with local taxing entities.
  • Neighborhood program. 2020 S.B. 112 created the community enhancement program for nearby neighborhoods, with reports to legislators. It passed 28-0 and 70-1.
  • Loan and spending checks. 2023 required a procurement policy and Executive Appropriations approval for loans. 2024 barred paying certain developer costs.
  • Contaminated land. 2024 barred distribution centers on contaminated land (changed in 2025 for privately owned land).
  • Yearly review. Since 2025, the board must review UIPA's law every year and recommend changes to the Legislature.
  • Data centers and disclosure. 2026 H.B. 507 limits local data center incentives. 2026 S.B. 206 requires disclosure before tax increment deals and bonds.
  • Consent. A county or city must still consent in writing before UIPA creates a project area on its land.
